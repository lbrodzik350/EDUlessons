School Technology Leadership and Student Data Privacy

Know your support team. Make informed decisions. Protect student information.

Digital learning is a shared responsibility. In this lesson, you will explore the professionals who support your classroom and two federal privacy laws that shape how schools use educational technology.

Learning objectives
  • Distinguish instructional technology support from technical break/fix support.
  • Describe how school technology teams and advisory committees contribute to decisions.
  • Distinguish FERPA from COPPA and recognize potential privacy concerns.
  • Identify appropriate steps before introducing a new digital tool.

SECTION 1 OF 3

Your school technology support system

As a classroom teacher, you are not expected to be a technology expert. Knowing who to contact can make your job much easier. Not every district has these positions, and titles and responsibilities vary, but you will likely encounter some version of these roles.

Instructional support or break/fix?

Digital Teaching and Learning Specialist: Sometimes called a Technology Facilitator or Instructional Technology Coach. This person helps teachers use technology effectively for instruction, align lessons with the NC Digital Learning Standards, select resources, and support accessibility, differentiation and engagement. They may model lessons, coach teachers and provide professional development.

Technology Technician: Sometimes called an Area Technical Coordinator (ATC). This person typically provides break/fix support: repairing devices, troubleshooting network issues, maintaining equipment and installing approved software.

School Library Media Coordinator: An instructional partner who helps teachers and students find and evaluate information, develop research skills, practice digital citizenship and use print and digital resources responsibly.

These professionals work together. The technician helps technology function; the facilitator helps technology improve learning; the media coordinator supports information literacy and resource selection.

Media and Technology Advisory Committees (MTAC)

Who decides what technology policies a school should follow? Which tools should a school spend its money on? Some schools have a Media and Technology Advisory Committee (MTAC) to help address these questions. Not all schools have one, but collaboration among teachers, administrators and technology professionals can be valuable.

  • Recommend technology policies and responsible-use expectations.
  • Identify priorities for devices, digital resources and software purchases.
  • Consider accessibility, privacy and instructional value.
  • Identify professional development needs and gather teacher input.

MTACs are generally advisory. Final authority may rest with a principal, district leadership or local board of education, depending on the decision and local policies.

Imagine your school has $5,000

Should it purchase devices, renew software, buy research resources or provide teacher training? A committee can gather evidence about student needs and recommend the most useful investment. Your classroom experience matters in these decisions.

Knowledge check: Who helps with a broken display?

A Technology Technician or ATC typically provides break/fix support.

Knowledge check: Who helps plan a standards-aligned digital lesson?

A Digital Teaching and Learning Specialist or Technology Facilitator can help with instructional planning.

FERPA: Protecting student education records

Imagine you discover an AI tool that analyzes assessment results and recommends personalized learning activities. All you have to do is upload a spreadsheet containing student names and grades. Sounds helpful, right? But are you allowed to share that information?

What is FERPA?

The Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act protects student education records at covered educational institutions. Parents and eligible students have rights to inspect records, request amendments and exercise certain controls over disclosure. Schools generally need consent to disclose personally identifiable information from education records unless a permitted exception applies.

What information should you protect?

Education records can include grades, assessment results, attendance, student identifiers, disciplinary records and individualized education programs (IEPs). The context matters: information must be handled according to applicable law and district procedures.

Potential FERPA concerns in the classroom
SituationConcern
Uploading named grades to an unapproved AI toolPossible unauthorized disclosure of education records.
Posting a public list of student names and scoresDisclosure of academic information.
Sharing an IEP with an unauthorized personDisclosure of confidential education records.
Allowing an unapproved app to import gradebook dataThird-party access to protected records.
Sharing a spreadsheet of student records through a public linkUnauthorized access to student information.

Does FERPA mean we cannot use educational technology?

Absolutely not! Schools can work with educational technology vendors under appropriate conditions. For example, a provider may qualify under the school official exception if it performs a school function, is under the school's direct control regarding records, meets the school's criteria for a legitimate educational interest and complies with restrictions on use and redisclosure.

Before you share

Check district approval, use only necessary information, and avoid entering identifiable records into unapproved websites or AI tools. Consult your school technology team or administrator if you are unsure. Removing names alone may not make information non-identifiable.

Knowledge check: A teacher uploads identifiable assessment scores to an unapproved AI tool. What is the concern?

The teacher may have disclosed personally identifiable information from education records without authorization.

Knowledge check: Can every employee access every student's records?

No. Access under the school official exception requires a legitimate educational interest.

Knowledge check: Does paying for software automatically make its use FERPA compliant?

No. The school's use of the software and handling of records must meet applicable requirements.

SECTION 3 OF 3

COPPA: Protecting children online

You attend a conference and discover a free website that allows students to create animated stories. You cannot wait to use it with your second graders. Then you discover that students must create accounts with their names and email addresses. What should you do?

What is COPPA?

The Children's Online Privacy Protection Act and its implementing rule regulate how covered online services collect, use and disclose children's personal information.

Under 13

The age group specifically protected by COPPA. This makes the law especially relevant to elementary educators.

Covered operators generally must notify parents and obtain verifiable parental consent before collecting personal information from children, unless an applicable exception applies.

What counts as personal information?

Even if a site does not ask for a child's name, it may still collect personal information.

The amended COPPA Rule also includes biometric and government-issued identifiers. Certain persistent identifiers that recognize users over time can also be covered.

What could a COPPA violation look like?

Hypothetical examples involving covered online services
ScenarioPotential concern
A site collects second graders' names and emails without required consent.Unauthorized collection of personal information.
A reading app stores children's voice recordings without required notice or consent.Collection of recordings containing children's voices.
A game tracks children for personalized advertising without required consent.Commercial use of children's information.
An AI application collects children's photos without required consent.Collection of children's images.
An app collects precise location without required authorization.Collection of location information.

Familiar tools and age restrictions

Popular services may restrict younger users. For example, requiring children under 13 to independently use ChatGPT, create Discord accounts or create standard TikTok accounts may be inappropriate. These examples are not accusations that the companies violate COPPA. Verify current terms, educational account options and district approval. Never ask students to enter false birth dates to bypass restrictions.

Can schools provide consent on behalf of parents?

Under certain conditions, a school can authorize collection for a school-directed educational purpose. That authority is limited to the educational context and does not cover unrelated commercial purposes. The operator remains responsible for its COPPA obligations. Follow your district's approval procedures rather than independently authorizing new applications.

Real-world case: Edmodo

Edmodo was an educational platform for classroom communication and learning. In 2023, the FTC obtained an order addressing allegations that Edmodo improperly collected children's information, used it for advertising and shifted compliance obligations to schools. Educational branding alone does not guarantee appropriate privacy practices.

Read the FTC's Edmodo case announcement.

Before introducing a new digital tool

Questions to ask

  • Is it district approved and age appropriate?
  • What data does it collect and how is it used?
  • Is it accessible to students?
  • Does it support my learning objectives?

Privacy red flags

  • Age restrictions exclude your students.
  • Privacy or deletion policies are unclear.
  • Unnecessary data collection or targeted advertising.
  • Consent procedures are unclear.
Knowledge check: Can a teacher tell second graders to enter false birth dates?

No. Respect age restrictions and follow district procedures.

Knowledge check: Can COPPA apply to an app recording children's voices without names?

Yes. Recordings containing children's voices may constitute personal information.

Knowledge check: What should you do if a new app is not on the approved list?

Consult your Technology Facilitator or appropriate district personnel before asking students to use it.

Key takeaway

Just because a website is free, educational or unblocked on your school computer does not mean it is approved for student use. Know your technology support team and protect your students.
